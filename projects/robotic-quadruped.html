Project

Robotic Quadruped

A four-legged walking robot built with the robotics team. My part was assisting in the design of the power distribution board: the board every other subsystem plugs into for power, with one battery input, a fuse on every leg, a regulated bus, and four protected leg outputs.

Overview

The quadruped is a Columbia University Robotics Club subteam project. I helped with the design of the power distribution board (PDB), had the board manufactured by JLCPCB, and soldered and wired the components onto it.

The robot runs on a single battery pack that has to feed a drive motor in every leg alongside the compute and sensors. The PDB sits between the pack and everything else: one high-current input, protection and regulation in the middle, and four leg outputs, so every other board just plugs in and gets clean power.

Raw pack voltage comes in through an XT90 connector, each of the four leg branches gets its own fuse, the bus runs through a buck converter that drops it to the level the leg motors are rated for, and every leg passes through an ideal-diode IC so current can only flow out to the leg and never back into the bus. This is the second revision of the board.

Tech Stack

  • Altium
  • Power Distribution
  • Buck Converter
  • XT90
  • Blade Fuses
  • LM73100 Ideal Diode
  • Reverse-Polarity Protection

Key Features

  • J1 is an XT90 connector that lands the battery pack straight onto the main bus. It is the single high-current entry point for the whole robot.
  • Four fuses, one per leg branch, so a short or a stalled motor in one leg blows only that fuse and leaves the bus and the other three legs running.
  • Four output connectors, one wired to each leg of the quadruped, each carrying its own fused and diode-protected copy of the bus.
  • U1 is a buck converter that steps the pack voltage down to the level the RobStride leg motors are rated to accept, so the actuators see a regulated supply instead of raw battery voltage that sags and spikes under load.
  • Four ideal-diode ICs (LM73100) down the middle, one per leg output. They act as near-lossless one-way valves, so a leg braking or back-driving can’t push current back into the bus or into the other three legs, and they block reverse polarity if the battery is ever connected backwards.
  • Laid out in Altium with wide copper pours and short high-current paths running from the input, through the fuses and the regulator, out to the leg connectors.
